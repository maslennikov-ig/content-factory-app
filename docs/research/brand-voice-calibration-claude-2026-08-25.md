# Author-Independent Calibration and Cross-Lingual Validation of a Self-Referential Style-Verification Product: A Research Report

## TL;DR
- **Your core reasoning is sound but incomplete.** Using the 95th percentile of an author's own leave-one-out distances as a per-author threshold is a legitimate, literature-supported way to make the threshold author-relative — it is essentially the "intrinsic/unary" verification paradigm (Potha & Stamatatos; Halvani et al.). But it is not free: with a 15,000-character corpus you have very few leave-one-out samples, so the 95th percentile is a noisy order statistic that needs shrinkage, and it does nothing to calibrate against the *right adversary* (an LLM on your topics), which is exactly where your measure is weakest (66.7% vs 94.3%).
- **Almost none of your six frozen parameters (char 5-grams, top-400 n-grams, 10th/90th corridor, threshold floor, min-corpus rules, headline acceptance number) can be shown to generalise from one author in one language.** The literature is unanimous that optimal feature/profile settings vary by language, genre, and text length, and that Russian/English results do not transfer automatically. You must re-derive them on a cross-author development set, and treat "cost of language N" as an empirical question per language.
- **Three authors buys you a sanity check, not validation.** Published stylometry validates on tens to thousands of authors; PAN 2020 verification used 275,565 pairs. Three corpora can *falsify* a parameter (if it fails on three it will fail broadly) but cannot *confirm* generalisation. A defensible pilot is 3 languages × ~30–50 authors each; a defensible norm needs hundreds of authors per language.

## Key Findings

1. **The similarity threshold as the 95th percentile of leave-one-out distances is the strongest part of the design** — it corresponds to the accepted principle that verification thresholds should be author-relative, not global constants. The weakness is statistical: at 15k characters you have few LOO folds, so the percentile is high-variance and should be shrunk toward a cross-author prior (empirical-Bayes / James–Stein).

2. **Author-independent calibration is a solved problem in outline but not with your feature set.** The field's answer is (a) z-scoring/Delta against a *reference corpus*, (b) percentile norms over a *population of authors*, (c) the *impostors/General Impostors (GI)* framework which removes the fixed threshold by asking "is X more similar to Y than to random distractors," and (d) *probability calibration* (logistic/isotonic) on a *cross-author development set* — the approach PAN's top systems settled on, exemplified by Boenninghoff et al.'s PAN 2020 winner (overall 0.935) which separates a Bayes-factor scoring model from a calibration layer.

3. **License-clean reference corpora exist for all sixteen of your languages via multilingual collections, but the cleanest legal path for an AGPL-3.0 product is to distribute derived n-gram/frequency statistics, not text.** Leipzig, OSCAR, HPLT, CC-100, mC4, CulturaX, FineWeb-2 all carry caveats (see Details). Derived-statistics-only distribution (Google-Books-Ngram style) largely sidesteps the licensing of the underlying text.

4. **Cross-lingual transfer of the *method* is partial; the *parameters* must be re-validated per language.** mStyleDistance and MSR/multilingual-authorship-representation work show quality varies substantially by language and that low-resource languages behave differently. Adding language N means, at minimum, a reference corpus + re-validation, not just a word list.

5. **Genre/topic robustness is the best-studied risk.** Character n-grams are relatively robust but degrade cross-topic; function-word-only sets, text distortion (Stamatatos), and POSNoise (Halvani & Graner) are the standard corrections.

6. **Your right adversary — a competent LLM writing on the author's own topics — is understudied but the emerging evidence is encouraging: current AV methods reject LLM impersonations more often than they reject genuine different-author texts, largely because LLM output has higher lexical diversity/entropy.** Your 66.7% is low relative to this literature, suggesting your specific feature set is not exploiting the signals that survive this adversary.

## Details

### Q1. Author-independent calibration in stylometry and authorship verification

**ESTABLISHED.**

- **Burrows's Delta (Burrows 2002)** is the canonical author-independent normalisation: take the most frequent words, z-score each word's frequency *against the mean and standard deviation across a reference set of texts/authors*, and measure the mean absolute z-score difference. The z-scoring is exactly "how much does this author deviate from how most people write." Evert et al. (2017, *Digital Scholarship in the Humanities*) showed the effectiveness of Delta depends primarily on **vector normalisation**, and that **Cosine Delta** (L2-normalised vectors) is the most robust variant across languages; they proposed the "Key Profiles Hypothesis" that authors are distinguished by the overall pattern of deviation from the norm. Hoover (2004) showed using many more than 150 frequent words improves accuracy, and that removing personal pronouns and single-text-dominated words helps.
- **Reference set choice:** Evert et al. and later cross-domain work (Barlas & Stamatatos) find the **normalisation corpus is crucial** in cross-domain attribution — the mean/SD must come from a background corpus representative of the language/register, not from the single author. For Delta, ~3,000 most-frequent words has been found robust across languages (Evert et al. 2017).
- **Corpus size:** Delta works "at corpus sizes of thousands rather than millions": tests on Ancient Greek prose showed good results at 1,000/3,000/5,000-word samples with 100–500-word feature vectors, though performance drops within similar genres. This is directly relevant to your 15k–150k character (≈2,500–25,000 word) regime.
- **Threshold calibration on a cross-author development set + probability calibration:** The PAN authorship-verification shared tasks (2013–2015, then 2020–2024) are the primary source. PAN evaluates with **AUC, c@1** (accuracy variant rewarding non-answers), **F1, F0.5u** (rewards correctly deciding same-author cases; Bevendorff et al. 2019), and the complement of the **Brier score** (probability-calibration quality). The PAN 2020 winner, **Boenninghoff et al., reached an overall score of 0.935 that "significantly outperforms the runner-up"** (weerasinghe20, 0.902), explicitly separating a Bayes-factor scoring model from a calibration layer; its O2D2 system reported AUC 0.965, c@1 0.927, Brier 0.927, overall 0.932. PAN observed that several teams achieved strong AUROC/c@1 but poor Brier because they returned binary answers rather than calibrated probabilities.
- **Platt scaling vs isotonic regression:** Platt scaling (logistic/sigmoid, 2 parameters) is preferable when the calibration set is small (a few hundred points) or the distortion is sigmoid-shaped; **isotonic regression** is non-parametric and more powerful but overfits below ~1,000 calibration points (Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005: "When there are 1000 or more points in the calibration set, Isotonic Regression always yields performance as good as, or better than, Platt Scaling"). For your regime — few calibration cases per author — Platt-style/logistic calibration or Venn-Abers (which yields validity guarantees) is the safer default.
- **Empirical Bayes / James–Stein shrinkage (ESTABLISHED in statistics, RARE in stylometry):** James–Stein (1961) and its empirical-Bayes interpretation (Efron & Morris 1973) show that when estimating many means from small samples, shrinking each toward a common target dominates the raw per-item estimate. This is the correct statistical treatment of your per-author 95th-percentile threshold and your per-author n-gram frequencies on a 15k-char corpus: shrink each author's estimate toward the cross-author (or reference-corpus) mean, with intensity set by the ratio of within-author to between-author variance. Hausser & Strimmer (2009) give a closed-form James–Stein shrinkage estimator for cell/word frequencies — directly applicable to your top-400 n-gram profile.
- **The impostors method / General Impostors (GI) framework (Koppel & Winter 2014, JASIST 65(1):178–187; Seidman 2013; Kestemont et al. 2016):** This is the single most important pointer for you. Instead of a fixed threshold, GI asks whether the unknown text is more similar to the author's known text than to each of a set of **impostor/distractor** documents, repeated over many random feature subspaces (typically over most-frequent character 4-grams). A text is "by the author" only if it withstands random feature perturbation and beats the impostors in a large fraction of trials. **This substantially removes the need for a fixed similarity threshold** — the decision becomes a relative ranking against a population, which is inherently author- and language-adaptive. Potha & Stamatatos (2017) improved impostor selection (impostors should match the *suspect's known writing*, like a police line-up). Stamatatos (2016) concluded binary-extrinsic (impostors) methods tend to outperform other AV types.

**CONTESTED.**
- Whether a *fixed* similarity threshold can ever generalise across authors. Halvani, Graner & Vogel (2018, "Authorship Verification in the Absence of Explicit Features and Thresholds") argue you should derive thresholds *per verification case*; their unary/intrinsic view aligns with your per-author percentile. But Halvani et al. (2019) also show binary-intrinsic methods (GLAD, COAV) that learn a threshold on a cross-author training set often *outperform* unary ones — i.e., some cross-author calibration usually beats purely intrinsic thresholds. The tension: purely per-author is robust to unseen authors but leaves discriminative power on the table.
- Whether unmasking (Koppel & Schler 2004) works for short texts — empirically it does not (Sanderson & Guenter 2006), which matters because your texts are short.

**FOLKLORE.**
- "Char 5-grams / top-400 n-grams / 10th–90th corridor are good defaults." These are reasonable but there is no cross-author, cross-language evidence for these *specific* values; the literature's robust defaults are char 3-grams and ~3,000 MFW for Delta. Your specific numbers are single-author, single-language artefacts until shown otherwise.

**On your specific claim:** The reasoning that the 95th-percentile-of-LOO threshold "is per-author by construction and generalises" is *directionally correct* but has two holes: (1) at 15k characters the number of LOO folds is small, so the 95th percentile is a high-variance order statistic — shrink it toward a cross-author prior; (2) a threshold calibrated on the author's own (human, same-topic) texts says nothing about where an LLM-on-your-topics lands, which is your real failure mode.

### Q2. Reference corpora, per language, and AGPL-3.0 compatibility

The crucial legal insight first: **an AGPL-3.0 product distributes its "source"; embedding a third-party *text* corpus inside it forces you to satisfy that corpus's license for redistribution. Embedding only *derived statistics* (n-gram frequency tables, z-score means/SDs) is a different and usually much weaker constraint** — frequency lists are facts/derived data, analogous to Google Books Ngrams, and are typically not a substantial redistribution of the copyrighted expression. **Recommendation up front: ship derived n-gram/frequency tables, not corpora.** This is the single decision that most simplifies your licensing.

**Multilingual collections (ESTABLISHED facts):**
- **Leipzig Corpora Collection (Wortschatz):** >250 languages, standardized sizes 10k–1M+ sentences, sources = news / web / Wikipedia, sentences are **scrambled (randomised order) for copyright reasons** — which is actually *ideal* for building n-gram norms and sidesteps redistribution of running text. License varies by release/portal: several portal and CLARIN releases are **CC BY-NC 4.0** (e.g., the Arabic OSIAN release states "Creative Commons Attribution-Non-Commercial 4.0 International License - CC BY-NC 4.0"), while RDF/LOD pages state CC BY-SA 4.0. **NC would block commercial embedding of the text; but deriving frequency tables for internal norms, or a non-commercial product, is fine.** Covers all sixteen of your locales.
- **OSCAR / OSCAR-23.01:** web-crawled, ~150+ languages. License applies to *metadata/packaging*, not the underlying Common Crawl text; provenance ambiguous. Research-friendly, commercial redistribution of text risky.
- **CC-100, mC4:** Common-Crawl-derived; same "license covers packaging not content" caveat; broad language coverage.
- **CulturaX (167 languages), HPLT v1.2/v2 (~190+ languages), MADLAD-400 (419), Glot500/GlotCC:** all CC/Common-Crawl-derived; HPLT and CulturaX are the strongest current large multilingual sets but carry the same underlying-text licensing ambiguity.
- **FineWeb-2 (Hugging Face, Dec 2024):** "roughly 20 terabytes, across 5 billion documents, with over 3 trillion words," organised into **1,868 language-script pairs**, released under the **Open Data Commons Attribution License (ODC-By) v1.0** and "also subject to CommonCrawl's Terms of Use." This is the most permissive large multilingual option, but note two facts: **English is NOT included in FineWeb-2** (use the separate FineWeb for English), and as a Common-Crawl derivative the ODC-By license covers the database, not each underlying document.

**Short-form/social corpora (ESTABLISHED):**
- **Blog Authorship Corpus (Koppel/Schler/Argamon):** the "collected posts of 19,320 bloggers gathered from blogger.com in August 2004... a total of 681,288 posts and over 140 million words - or approximately 35 posts and 7250 words per person." License: "may be freely used for non-commercial research purposes." Closest public analogue to your "personal diary" author type — but NC and non-commercial-only.
- **Webis-TLDR-17 (Reddit):** 3,848,330 posts, avg 270 words, with author IDs, released **CC BY 4.0** — usable for cross-author verification norms and commercially cleaner than most.
- **Pushshift Reddit:** very large, but redistribution now legally fraught after Reddit API changes.
- **Twitter/X academic corpora:** X's terms now prohibit redistribution of tweet text (hydration-only), so these are effectively unusable for an embedded product.
- **Language-specific social corpora exist but are small:** BN-AuthProf (Bengali, 300 authors, 30,131 Facebook statuses); an Arabic Twitter stylometry corpus (20,357 tweets, 134 users); Greek tweets (Mikros & Perifanos). These give existence proof but are too small for stable norms.

**Per-language verdict (candid):** For **en, de, es, fr, it, pt, ru, zh, ja, ko, ar, tr, vi** — license-clean *derived-statistics* norms are readily buildable from Leipzig + FineWeb-2 (+ FineWeb for English) + Wikipedia. For **bn** — thin; Leipzig + FineWeb-2 exist but social-register norms are weak. For **he** — Leipzig + Hebrew Wikipedia + OSCAR/HPLT exist; social-register thin. For **ka (Georgian)** — the weakest: Leipzig has Georgian, FineWeb-2 and CulturaX/HPLT include it, but it is genuinely low-resource and social-register norms are essentially unavailable. **This is a real product decision: ka and bn norms will be lower quality, and you should say so to users.**

**How many authors / how much text for a stable norm (ESTABLISHED-ish):** Delta-type norms stabilise with a few dozen texts but the *reference distribution* (mean/SD per feature) benefits from hundreds of authors. PAN norms use tens of thousands of pairs. For percentile norms ("this author jokes more than most") you want the population distribution of the statistic across **at least ~100–300 authors** per language to make the 10th/90th percentiles stable.

**Biases from web-scraped norms (ESTABLISHED):** Kreutzer et al. (2022, "Quality at a Glance: An Audit of Web-Crawled Multilingual Datasets") documented that web-crawled multilingual corpora have severe quality problems for mid/low-resource languages — misclassified languages, pornographic/boilerplate content, machine-translated text. A norm built from such data will (a) over-represent news/formal register vs your social/diary register, (b) contain MT-contaminated "average," and (c) be much noisier for ka/bn. Register mismatch (news norm vs social author) is the dominant bias.

### Q3. How many authors to validate rather than fit

**ESTABLISHED numbers (what published work actually uses):**
- Burrows (2002): 25 poets. Hoover (2004): tested on dozens. Evert et al. (2017): multiple corpora, dozens of authors each, 3+ languages.
- Koppel/Schler/Argamon and Narayanan et al. (2012): scaled to **10,000–100,000 authors** for attribution.
- PAN cross-domain attribution (2018–2019): up to 20 candidate authors per problem, 5 languages (en, fr, it, pl, es).
- PAN verification (2020–2021): fanfiction, **278,169 documents / 41k–278k authors**; the **PAN 2020 large training set comprised 275,565 verification pairs (147,778 same-author + 127,787 different-author) across 1,600 fandoms**; test sets with tens of thousands of authors.
- Surveying-stylometry benchmarks (Neal et al. 2017): performance analysis on **1,000 authors, 14 algorithms**.

**Cross-author protocols (ESTABLISHED):** leave-one-author-out; held-out author sets (PAN's open-set design: test authors and topics disjoint from training); cross-topic and cross-genre splits (Stamatatos 2013; Sapkota et al. 2014; the Topic Confusion Task, Altakrori et al. 2021). The gold standard is **open-set, cross-topic, leave-author-out** so that neither the author nor the topic is seen in tuning.

**Between-author variance (ESTABLISHED):** considered large and normal — PAN overview papers repeatedly note per-author and per-fandom performance varies widely; model rankings are "dataset-specific and not generalizable" (Brad et al. 2022 reversed the PAN'21 difficulty ordering with different splits). This is precisely why one author cannot certify a parameter.

**What three corpora buys you (candid):** With three authors you can (1) *falsify* a parameter — if char 5-grams / top-400 fail to separate any of the three from impostors, they will fail generally; (2) detect gross overfitting to the original Russian author; (3) do a rough cross-language smoke test *if the three are in three languages*. What three **cannot** do: estimate between-author variance, set stable population percentiles (10th/90th from 3 points is meaningless), or certify the headline acceptance number. Three is a debugging set, not a validation set.

### Q4. Cross-lingual transfer of the method itself

**ESTABLISHED (per-language numbers):**
- **mStyleDistance (Qiu et al., Findings of ACL 2025, arXiv:2502.15168)** trained style embeddings in **nine languages: Arabic, German, Spanish, French, Hindi, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Chinese.** On the PAN 2013–2015 AV task (ROC-AUC), per-language averages: mStyleDistance **Greek 0.64, Spanish 0.73, Dutch 0.60, overall 0.66**, beating English-only StyleDistance (0.52/0.62/0.62/0.59) and Wegmann/CISR (0.56/0.67/0.59/0.61) overall — **but losing on Dutch** to English StyleDistance (0.62), which the authors attribute to West-Germanic proximity to English ("Dutch shows similar performance to English STYLEDISTANCE... the linguistic proximity (West Germanic roots)... helps STYLEDISTANCE to generalize to Dutch"). Crucially, individual per-dataset AUCs swing wildly (mStyleDistance PAN13 Greek = **0.41**, i.e. worse than chance, vs PAN15 Greek = 0.58). The authors concede in Limitations that their evaluation languages "are not really low-resource, so our evaluations may not reflect performance in languages with less resources."
- **Multilingual Authorship Representation / MSR (Kim et al. 2025, arXiv:2509.16531):** trained on **over 4.5M authors across 36 languages (body text: 35 seen languages, 10 seen domains; total pool 59 languages / 17 domains)**; non-English data is Wikipedia-only. It beats monolingual baselines in **21 of 22 non-English languages, average Recall@8 +4.85%, max +15.91% (Georgian)**. **Low-resource languages benefit most**: R@8 improves >15% for Georgian (56.82→72.73, 176 authors) and Kazakh (43.00→58.50, 200 authors), while high-resource French (8,944 authors) is essentially flat (55.48→55.52) and **Japanese is the one language where multilingual training loses (52.45→51.77)**. Two techniques: **probabilistic content masking** (optimal 20% mask rate) and **language-aware batching** (">6% gains in both seen and unseen languages, while incurring less than a 1% drop in seen English domains"). The paper documents the core data asymmetry verbatim: "While English corpora used in Rivera-Soto et al. (2021) include up to **1.1 million authors spanning three domains**, most existing **non-English datasets contain only a few hundred authors from a single domain**." STEB (2026) independently finds "multilingual style embeddings underperform their English counterparts... cross-lingual style [is] an open problem."

**CONTESTED:** whether a calibration procedure validated on one language transfers. The evidence says the *architecture* transfers with degradation, but the *operating point* (threshold, calibration curve) does not — per-dataset AUC swings and the Dutch/Japanese exceptions show language-specific behaviour. No paper demonstrates a fixed threshold transferring across languages.

**Practical answer to your question:** Adding language N means **more than a word list**: at minimum a reference corpus (for the norm), re-derivation of the percentile corridor and threshold behaviour, and a per-language validation pass. The feature *extractor* (char n-grams) transfers; the *calibration* does not. Budget per-language validation, not per-language localization.

### Q5. Robustness to genre and topic

**ESTABLISHED:**
- **Character n-grams are relatively robust but topic-sensitive.** Stamatatos (2013, *J. Law & Policy* 21:421–439) showed char n-grams outperform frequent-word representations when train/test differ in genre/topic, but performance still drops cross-topic and cross-genre (cross-genre worse than cross-topic because genre intermingles with personal style). Sapkota et al. (2015) "not all character n-grams are created equal" — prefix/suffix/space n-grams (affix- and punctuation-related) carry more style and less topic than word-internal ones.
- **What loses robustness first:** content-bearing features — word unigrams, content-word n-grams, and word-internal character n-grams that map to topic words. Function words, punctuation n-grams, and POS/syntactic features are the most topic-stable (the Topic Confusion Task, Altakrori et al. 2021, found POS-tag stylometric features least susceptible to topic variation).
- **Corrections (ESTABLISHED):**
  - **Text distortion (Stamatatos 2017, EACL; 2018, JASIST 69(3):461–473):** replace every word not among the top-k most frequent (in a reference corpus) with asterisks, keeping punctuation/capitalisation. Improves cross-topic and cross-genre attribution. Note: this *requires a reference corpus* — again pointing to Q2.
  - **POSNoise (Halvani & Graner 2021):** replace content tokens (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) with POS tags, keep function words/punctuation. More selective than text distortion; widely adopted in forensic AV (Nini et al. 2026 use it for content masking before likelihood-ratio estimation).
  - **Function-word-only feature sets** (Mosteller & Wallace lineage; Kestemont 2014): topic-agnostic by design.
  - **Domain adaptation / normalisation-corpus choice** (Barlas & Stamatatos 2020): the normalisation corpus dominates cross-domain performance.

**Direct implication for your product:** your authors "write about releases one week and a holiday the next" — this is exactly cross-topic within-author variation. Your LOO distances will be inflated by topic shifts, widening the corridor and possibly the 95th-percentile threshold. Apply POSNoise or text distortion *before* extracting char 5-grams, and compute the norm on distorted text, or your threshold will absorb topic variance and lose discriminative power against the real adversary.

### Q6. The right adversary — human author vs. LLM-on-the-author's-topics

**ESTABLISHED (and directly relevant to your 66.7%):**
- The field distinguishes four problems (survey: "Authorship Attribution in the Era of LLMs," arXiv:2408.08946): human attribution, LLM-detection (human vs machine), LLM-attribution (which model), and human-LLM co-authorship. Your problem — "did THIS person write it, or an LLM imitating them on their topics" — is a hybrid of human verification and machine-negative detection, and is genuinely understudied relative to generic human-vs-machine.
- **The encouraging headline result: LLM impersonation does *not* currently evade competent AV.** "Authorship Impersonation via LLM Prompting does not Evade Authorship Verification Methods" (2026, arXiv:2603.29454) tested n-gram tracing, Ranking-Based Impostors, LambdaG, AdHominem, LUAR, STAR against GPT-4o impersonations across genres in a likelihood-ratio framework: "LLM-generated texts failed to sufficiently replicate authorial individuality to bypass established AV systems," and **some methods rejected impersonations *more* confidently than genuine different-author negatives.** The stated mechanism: LLM text has **higher lexical diversity and entropy** than the target human. Twitter/social impersonations were the *easiest* to reject; email (Enron) the hardest.
- "Catch Me If You Can? Not Yet" (arXiv:2509.14543): even 5-shot prompted LLMs imitating everyday authors are separable; AV accuracy on Reddit-style short text is far from perfect (Reddit 5-shot ~49–66%; Blog even lower), consistent with your 66.7%.
- Stylometry distinguishes human vs LLM in short samples (Przystalski et al. 2025, *Expert Systems with Applications*; a PLOS One Japanese study finds humans struggle but stylometry succeeds).

**CONTESTED:**
- Whether this resilience survives *continuation* attacks. "How Well Do LLMs Imitate Human Writing Style?" (arXiv:2509.24930) found that when an LLM *continues* a text whose first half establishes the author's stylistic manifold, four of five models "achieved at least 99.9% accuracy with verifier confidence saturated at 100%" — outputs became "virtually indistinguishable." So if your adversary has a genuine sample to prime on, detectability collapses. This is the scenario your product most needs to worry about.
- Whether the answer differs by language: under-studied. Most machine-negative AV work is English; the multilingual picture is largely open (STEB, MSR).

**Diagnosis of your numbers:** 94.3% (vs a human writing technical docs) is normal for a decent measure. 66.7% (vs LLM-on-topic) is *low relative to the 2026 impersonation literature*, where robust methods reject most impersonations. That your threshold flags only 1 of 24 generated texts suggests your threshold is calibrated to human same-author variance and simply sits in the wrong place for the machine adversary — the LLM texts fall *inside* your corridor. The literature's fix is not a better threshold but **better features** (the entropy/lexical-diversity signal, function-word and punctuation distributions, and impostor-relative scoring), and adding **LLM-generated negatives to your development set** so the operating point is calibrated against the actual adversary.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Reframe the threshold (immediate).**
Keep the per-author 95th-percentile-of-LOO threshold, but (a) **shrink it** toward a cross-author prior using James–Stein / empirical-Bayes (Hausser–Strimmer closed form for the n-gram frequencies; shrink the percentile toward the pooled cross-author percentile weighted by number of LOO folds); (b) add an **impostors/GI decision layer** so the accept/reject is relative to a distractor population, not an absolute cut. Benchmark: shrinkage should reduce threshold variance across resampled LOO folds by a visible margin without lowering same-author acceptance.

**Stage 1 — Build a cross-author development set (weeks).**
Assemble **≥30–50 authors per language** for your three pilot languages (choose them to span your genres: technical, diary, corporate). Use leave-one-author-out + cross-topic splits. Re-derive *every* frozen parameter (n-gram order, profile size, corridor percentiles, threshold floor, min-corpus rule, headline number) on this set. Passing bar: each parameter's chosen value should sit in the flat region of the performance curve across authors *and* languages, not at a single-author optimum.

**Stage 2 — Add the right adversary to calibration (weeks, highest priority).**
Generate LLM-on-topic negatives for each development author (0-shot and 5-shot; include a *continuation* attack). Calibrate the operating point with **logistic/Platt scaling or Venn-Abers** (not isotonic — too little data) on a held-out cross-author set that *includes machine negatives*. Add entropy/lexical-diversity and function-word/punctuation features, which the literature shows survive this adversary. Passing bar: machine-negative rejection ≥ the ~90%+ seen in the 2026 impersonation literature at equal length; report the continuation-attack number separately (expect it to be much worse).

**Stage 3 — Topic-robustness pass.**
Apply **POSNoise or text distortion** before feature extraction; recompute norms on distorted text. Passing bar: within-author cross-topic LOO distance distribution tightens (10th/90th corridor narrows) relative to raw text.

**Stage 4 — Per-language rollout.**
For each new language: build a **derived-statistics reference norm** (n-gram/frequency tables only, from Leipzig + FineWeb-2 [+ FineWeb for English] + Wikipedia — never redistribute text) and run a scaled-down Stage 1–2 validation (≥100–300 authors for stable percentiles where obtainable). Ship ka and bn with an explicit lower-confidence flag; do not claim the headline number for them until validated. Threshold that changes this plan: if a language cannot reach ≥~0.80 AUC on same-author-vs-different-human at your text lengths, do not ship automated verdicts for it — offer scores only.

**Licensing:** default to **shipping derived statistics, not corpora**; if you must embed text, use **FineWeb-2 (ODC-By, but note English excluded and CommonCrawl ToU applies)** or **CC BY** Leipzig releases, and avoid CC BY-NC releases (much of Leipzig; the Blog Authorship Corpus) and Twitter/Reddit-API-restricted text in a commercial AGPL product.

## What would falsify the approach
- If, on the cross-author development set, the per-author 95th-percentile threshold's false-accept rate against **human different-author** negatives varies by more than a few points across authors *after shrinkage*, the "it generalises by construction" claim fails.
- If char 5-grams / top-400 profiles do not sit in a flat optimum region across authors and ≥3 languages, those parameters are overfit and must be re-chosen.
- If machine-negative rejection stays near your current 66.7% after adding entropy/function-word features, impostor scoring, and adversary-inclusive calibration, then your representation — not your threshold — is inadequate for the LLM adversary, and the product's core value proposition (distinguishing the author from an LLM on their topics) is unmet.
- If a low-resource language (ka, bn) cannot produce a stable population norm (10th/90th percentiles unstable across resamples), you cannot honestly offer "more/less than most people" statements there.

## What the literature does NOT answer
- **The exact right-adversary operating point for short social text per language.** Machine-negative AV is nascent and overwhelmingly English; there is no published, per-language calibration for "human vs LLM-on-their-topics" at 15k–150k-character corpora.
- **How the continuation/priming attack interacts with per-author thresholds** — the one study showing near-perfect LLM mimicry via continuation is recent and not integrated with threshold-calibration practice.
- **Stable population norms for Georgian and Bengali social/short-form text** — the corpora do not exist at the size needed; this is a genuine gap, not an oversight.
- **Whether empirical-Bayes shrinkage of per-author percentile thresholds improves real AV** — the statistics are established but, to our knowledge, no stylometry paper has specifically validated shrinking a per-author LOO-percentile threshold.
- **The minimum corpus-size rules** (your 15k floor) have no cross-author, cross-language empirical grounding in the literature; published short-text AV results are noisy exactly in this regime and no consensus floor exists.

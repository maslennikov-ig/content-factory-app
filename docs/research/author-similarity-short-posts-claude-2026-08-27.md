# Measuring Author Similarity for Short Social Posts: Seven Decisions

**Scope note:** Read-only research, current date 2026-08-27. Verdicts stated before evidence. English-only evidence is labelled as such and not generalised to Russian or to 16 locales. Where the literature has no answer, I say so. Licences are quoted from governing documents; where I could reach only a secondary summary I say so beside it.

---

## Q1. Can the Impostors working point be normalised so it means the same thing for different authors?

**Verdict: No — not from the authorship-verification (AV) literature. Per-author *score-level* normalisation of the Impostors / General Imposters (GI) output does not exist in the published AV literature. It is standard practice in speaker verification (Z-norm, T-norm, S-norm, adaptive s-norm), but transferring it to AV is an untested analogy, not a documented method. What the AV literature does offer is (a) feature-level z-scoring, (b) per-*problem* impostor selection, and (c) rank-based within-trial normalisation — none of which makes a fixed working point comparable across authors the way your table demands. Actionable conclusion: there is no off-the-shelf normalisation; you must build a per-author null distribution yourself and borrow the speaker-verification recipe, labelling it clearly as an unvalidated import.**

Evidence, verified against primary text (via a targeted deep dive on the primary PDFs):

- **Koppel & Winter 2014 (JASIST 65(1):178–187, https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~koppel/papers/impostors-journal-revised2-140213.pdf)** use a single global threshold σ*, tuned only by the prior probability of same-author, never per author. They explicitly note that "a single uniform threshold for all pairs is not likely to work especially well" — but their remedy is feature sub-sampling and genre/topic-matched impostor construction, not score normalisation. They credit the background-set idea to speaker identification (Reynolds 1995) but adapt only the "lineup" concept, writing that as far as they know it "has not been previously used for authorship attribution." Their threshold table varies σ* only with the prior (prior 0.1→σ*≈0.30 … prior 0.9→σ*≈0.01), not by author.
- **stylo's `imposters()` z-scoring is on FEATURES, not the output score** (https://rdrr.io/cran/stylo/man/imposters.html, reference manual built 2024-05-29). The `z.scores.both.sets` argument reads: "many distance measures convert input variables into z-scores before computing any distances." The output is "a single score … between 0 and 1." So the z-scoring in the reference implementation is a property of the Delta kernel, not a per-author score calibration. The function header confirms it "is an implementation of the 2nd order verification system known as the General Imposters framework (GI), and introduced by Koppel and Winter (2014)."
- **Potha & Stamatatos 2017 ("An Improved Impostors Method", CLEF 2017, LNCS 10456, pp. 138–144, https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-65813-1_14)** do two relevant things. (1) Per-*problem* impostor selection: "Instead of selecting the impostor documents for each verification problem randomly, we propose to select the external documents with the highest min-max similarity score with respect to the known documents" — the police-lineup metaphor ("in a police lineup it doesn't make sense to draw the suspects from the general population"). (2) They acknowledge raw scores are incomparable across sets — "the absolute similarity score may significantly differ when different sets of documents are used" — and their fix is **ranking**, not per-author z-scoring: they use the rank position of the known document among impostors.
- **PAN cross-domain AV overviews (Kestemont et al. 2020, CEUR 2696 paper_264, https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2696/paper_264.pdf; 2021, CEUR 2936 paper-147)** use a fixed global 0.5 threshold for every problem, with 0.5 doubling as the non-answer. "Calibration" there means per-*system* training on the shared calibration set, never per-author. The only score-distribution concerns discussed are topic reliance and "number heaping" (modes at 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1), not per-author separating power.

**On per-author variance of separating power:** the AV literature does not publish a systematic study of how GI separation varies by author under identical parameters. Your specific observation — 57.2 points of headroom for author A versus 16.1 for author C under identical method/impostors/parameters — has no published characterisation. Treat your three-author table as a novel measurement, not a reproduction of anything in the literature.

**Speaker-verification analogue (report honestly as "what it would take"):** adaptive s-norm (AS-norm) is designed for exactly this problem — it normalises a trial score by the mean and standard deviation of that speaker's scores against a cohort of similar impostors, per speaker. Matějka, Novotný, Plchot, Burget, Diez Sánchez & Černocký, "Analysis of Score Normalization in Multilingual Speaker Recognition" (Interspeech 2017, pp. 1567–1571, https://www.fit.vut.cz/research/group/speech/public/publi/2017/matejka_interspeech2017_IS170803.pdf): "The best performing one for a PLDA classifier is an adaptive s-norm with 30% relative improvement over the system without any score normalization." Their analysis uses a top-200 selected cohort (NIST SRE 2016), and reports that the adaptive cohort "in 68% contain recordings from the same language and in 92% of the same gender as the enrollment and test recordings" — which also directly supports the Q5 language-matched-cohort point. The mechanical transfer to your case: for each author, build a null distribution of Impostors scores (author's held-out posts and/or the impostor cohort), then z-score every candidate's Impostors score against that per-author null (mean, sd), expressing every working point in per-author standard-deviation units. **This is documented only in speaker verification. No AV paper validates it. Label it as an import.**

**What would overturn this verdict:** a published AV paper (not speaker verification) applying per-author or per-problem score-distribution normalisation (a Z/T/S-norm analogue) to Impostors/GI *outputs* and reporting comparability across authors.

## Q2. What character n-gram configuration is right for short social posts?

**Verdict: Your 5-gram choice is defensible and near the empirically supported band (3–5 is the workhorse range); "top-400 by frequency" is NOT supported as an optimum by any primary source — it is your hypothesis, and the evidence suggests it is both too small and the wrong selection principle. The strongest, most transferable finding is Sapkota et al. 2015: most of character n-grams' discriminative power comes from *affix* and *punctuation* n-grams, not arbitrary top-frequency ones. The optimum shifts across writing systems, and evidence is thin-to-absent for non-alphabetic scripts.**

Evidence:

- **Sapkota, Bethard, Montes-y-Gómez & Solorio 2015 ("Not All Character N-grams Are Created Equal", NAACL 2015, N15-1010, pp. 93–102, https://aclanthology.org/N15-1010/)**: verbatim, "character n-grams that capture information about affixes and punctuation account for almost all of the power of character n-grams as features." This argues against pure top-frequency selection, which mixes in thematic-content n-grams that hurt cross-topic robustness. In cross-topic replications (Markov et al.), restricting to affix+punctuation typed n-grams yields **+4.7% (SVM) and +5.9% (MNB)** accuracy over undifferentiated n-grams. Evidence is English (Guardian, CCAT).
- **Stamatatos 2013 ("On the Robustness of Authorship Attribution Based on Character N-gram Features", J. Law & Policy 21(2), https://brooklynworks.brooklaw.edu/jlp/vol21/iss2/7/)**: character n-grams are the most robust feature under cross-topic/cross-genre conditions; 3–4-grams are the standard robust choices for English. On feature-set size, under cross-topic/genre conditions "the performance of an SVM steadily increases until around 3,500 features and then decreases dramatically" — i.e., the useful feature budget is on the order of thousands, not 400, but with a genuine ceiling beyond which more features hurt.
- **Feature-set size for your top-400 claim:** PAN and stylometry work routinely use far more than 400 features (e.g., the ACM SAC 2023 English/Russian study used 5,000 n-grams of length 3–8; GI randomises over large feature spaces). **No primary source establishes 400 as an optimum — it is a hypothesis, and no answer exists for the precise "top-400" figure.**
- **Cross-script:** the ACM SAC 2023 study ("Impact of Character n-grams Attention Scores for English and Russian", https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3555776.3577856) evaluated Cyrillic (Taiga) vs Latin (Reuters_50_50) and found n-gram importance differs by language. The affix+punctuation advantage is not universal: replications note it "does not seem to be valid for the Portuguese language," and a Turkish study observes "what may be true about this feature type for English (Stamatatos, 2013) may not be true for Turkish." For non-alphabetic scripts (your CJK locale), a ~600–900-character window holds far more tokens per character, and **no primary source I found measures optimal character n-gram size for CJK short social posts** — a genuine gap.

**Assumption stated:** I read "roughly 600–900 characters" as the length of an individual candidate text, not of the whole profile.

**What would overturn this:** a controlled study on 600–900-char social posts showing top-N-frequency selection at N≈400 beats affix/punctuation-typed selection at equal or larger N, per script.

## Q3. Minimum corpus in characters below which short-form AV is unreliable?

**Verdict: 15,000 characters is defensible as a hard floor but genuinely marginal, and the supporting evidence is about attribution of literary/edited prose in English, German, Polish, Latin — not Russian Telegram posts. Eder's canonical result is ~5,000 *words* per sample for stable attribution, which at ~6 characters/word in Russian is ≈30,000 characters — roughly double your gate. Your gate admits texts at the edge; confidence rises steeply with corpus size, and your three real authors (107k–754k) are comfortably above it. Recommend treating 15k–30k as a low-confidence band and pushing the effective floor toward 30k where possible.**

Evidence:

- **Eder 2015 ("Does size matter? Authorship attribution, small samples, big problem", Digital Scholarship in the Humanities 30(2):167–182)**: minimal sample length varied "from 2,500 words (Latin prose) to 5,000 or so words (in most cases, including English, German, Polish, and Hungarian novels)"; below that, classifiers become "blind." Also a methodological finding you can exploit: "randomly excerpted 'bags of words' turned out to be much more effective than … original sequences of words." Literary prose, not social media — label accordingly.
- **Bevendorff, Stein, Hagen & Potthast 2019 ("Generalizing Unmasking for Short Texts", NAACL-HLT 2019, N19-1068, pp. 654–659, https://aclanthology.org/N19-1068/)**: verification "of texts as short as four printed pages," at which length "it achieves accuracies of 75–80%" — i.e., short-form verification is possible but noticeably less reliable than long-form. English-language authors, similar genre/publication period.
- **Impostors specifically** was designed on blog-length texts; Koppel & Winter note unmasking fails on short inputs, which motivated the impostors approach — but they published no character-count reliability floor for the social-post regime.

There is **no primary source stating a character floor specifically for Russian Telegram-style posts.** Your 15,000 is below Eder's word-count-derived equivalent; defensible only as a hard minimum, not a comfort zone.

**What would overturn this:** a Russian/Cyrillic social-media AV study reporting reliable verification below ~15k characters of author material, or a learning curve showing your relative-feature AUC of 0.86–0.88 holds at 15k.

## Q4. What completes a decision rule whose first half is the 95th percentile of the author's own distances?

**Verdict: Complete it with an operating point chosen on a labelled adversarial set (author-held-out vs generated text) — i.e., pick the threshold on the ROC/DET curve at your chosen error trade-off, and report the two error rates as a confusion matrix in plain language ("false accept rate" and "false reject rate"). The 95th percentile alone is a one-sided tolerance and, as the previous round concluded, says nothing about how close a stranger comes. The accepted machinery is c@1 with a non-answer option, plus AUC and Brier for calibration — the PAN evaluation family.**

Evidence:

- **PAN AV evaluation family** (pan.webis.de/clef20 … clef25): systems output a bounded [0,1] score against a **0.5 threshold** and may abstain — 0.5 = non-answer. Metrics: **ROC-AUC** (ranking), **c@1** ("a modified accuracy score that assigns non-answers … the average accuracy of the remaining cases"), **F0.5u** ("a modified F0.5 measure … that treats non-answers … as false negatives"; puts more weight on deciding same-author cases correctly), and **Brier** (probability calibration). c@1 is the honest accuracy measure when abstention is allowed.
- **Two-error report for a non-technical user:** frame as a 2×2 table on real counts — of 100 genuine author texts, how many were wrongly rejected (false reject / miss); of 100 impostor/generated texts, how many were wrongly accepted (false accept / false alarm). The PAN evaluator emits a confusion matrix with true/false positive/negative rates directly. Do not report a single accuracy number.
- **Setting the point against a real adversary:** your adversary is LLM-generated imitation. Threshold the *relative* feature you already measured (AUC 0.86–0.88); build the curve from author-held-out (positives) vs generated (negatives) and pick the operating point at the false-accept rate the product can tolerate. This is the "builder-breaker" adversarial framing PAN's Voight-Kampff task adopted for machine-vs-human verification (2024–2026).

**What would overturn this:** evidence that a fixed one-sided percentile achieves comparable error control to an ROC-selected operating point on adversarial (generated) negatives.

## Q5. Does a calibration obtained in one language transfer to another?

**Verdict: The representation transfers; the operating point does not. This confirms the previous round's conclusion, now with citations. Multilingual authorship representations show real but degraded cross-lingual transfer — worst across different scripts and language families — so a threshold set on one language will not hold on another. A 16-locale product must set and validate the operating point per locale (or at least per script family), even if it shares one representation/model.**

Evidence:

- **"Authorship Attribution in Multilingual Machine-Generated Texts" (arXiv:2508.01656, 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.01656)**: "current authorship attribution methods struggle to generalize across dissimilar language families or writing scripts, as performances are heavily affected by the linguistic properties of the target languages." They deliberately chose English/Spanish/Russian as "the most popular languages with the two most representative scripts … Latin and Cyrillic" — direct evidence that Cyrillic↔Latin transfer is degraded.
- **"Leveraging Multilingual Training for Authorship Representation" (arXiv:2509.16531, 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16531)**: authorship-representation models "can indeed enjoy cross-lingual transfer from multilingual training … surprising given the syntactic and grammatical diversity," but gains are uneven per language — the embedding transfers; performance (and thus any fixed threshold) does not.
- **Speaker-verification analogue** (Matějka et al. 2017): the best score-normalisation cohorts match language/channel (68% same-language cohort selection), reinforcing that the *operating point* is language-dependent even when the representation is shared. Flagged as analogy, not AV evidence.

**What a 16-locale product must do:** calibrate the threshold (per Q4) separately per locale, on locale-specific author-vs-generated data. Do not port author A's Russian working point to a Latin or CJK locale.

**What would overturn this:** a study measuring AV *operating-point* transfer (not just AUC) across scripts and finding a single threshold holds within tolerance.

## Q6. Is there a licence-clean, author-preserving corpus of short social texts, commercial use permitted?

**Verdict: Yes for English, with sharp caveats — the best licence-clean, author-labelled options are (a) the Webis-TLDR-17 Reddit corpus (dataset metadata: CC BY-4.0, `author` field present) and (b) the Stack Exchange data dump (CC BY-SA 4.0, which explicitly permits commercial use). The Blog Authorship Corpus is NOT commercial-clean (non-commercial only). All three carry a platform-Terms-of-Service-vs-licence tension you must weigh. For Russian/other locales there is no answer — no licence-clean, author-preserving short-social corpus at scale that I could verify. Quotes below; do not adopt on my summary alone — re-read the quoted governing text.**

Licences quoted with governing document and date:

- **Blog Authorship Corpus — NOT usable commercially.** Governing text (Koppel's page, https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~koppel/BlogCorpus.htm): "The corpus may be freely used for **non-commercial research purposes**." (19,320 bloggers, author-labelled, English, ~681,288 posts.) **Reject for a commercial product.** The Hugging Face mirror (barilan/blog_authorship_corpus) lists "License: unknown" — exactly the kind of secondary mis-statement to distrust; the governing source is explicit and restrictive.
- **Stack Exchange dump — CC BY-SA 4.0, commercial permitted, two catches.** Governing licence text (archived license.txt, https://ia600508.us.archive.org/30/items/stackexchange/license.txt): all content is "cc-wiki licensed, intended to be shared and remixed," requiring attribution (visual indication, hyperlink to source, author names shown). The ToS (quoted in the BigScience ROOTS card) grants Stack Overflow the right "to commercially exploit such Subscriber Content … pursuant to Creative Commons licensing terms (CC BY-SA 4.0)." **Catch 1 — ShareAlike:** BY-SA is copyleft; derivatives may need to carry the same licence, which can be incompatible with a closed product that redistributes text. **Catch 2 — ToS override:** since mid-2024 Stack Exchange gates the dump behind login and an agreement that the file is "for projects that do not include training a large language model" (https://devclass.com/2024/07/30/, 2024-07-30). The CC licence covers the *content*; the *access mechanism* now imposes an extra contractual restriction. Author identity preserved (per-user IDs). Q&A prose, multiple languages including Russian/Spanish/Japanese.
- **Webis-TLDR-17 (Reddit) — CC BY-4.0 per dataset metadata, author field present, commercial permitted under CC-BY.** Hugging Face card YAML (https://huggingface.co/datasets/webis/tldr-17): `license: - cc-by-4.0`; Google Dataset Search: "License Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0)." 3,848,330 posts, `author` field retained, English. **Catch:** CC-BY here is asserted by the dataset packagers over Reddit user content; Reddit's own API/ToS and users' underlying rights are a separate layer the CC-BY tag does not extinguish. Treat CC-BY as covering the corpus compilation, not a warranty over every post's copyright.
- **Telegram research corpora — mixed and mostly not clean.** TeraGram (arXiv:2605.15956, 2026) is ODC-By (attribution, commercial OK) but is a structural/longitudinal dataset, not an author-labelled prose corpus. A Telegram hate-speech corpus (PhilArchive) is CC-BY but single-channel and English. The Russian milblogger corpus (ScienceDirect S2352340925007371, 2025) is domain-specific war content. None is a general licence-clean, author-preserving short-post corpus in Russian at scale.

**Recommendation:** For licence-clean *calibration* data independent of your three authors, use **Webis-TLDR-17 (CC BY-4.0, author-preserving)** as the primary English calibration corpus, and hold **Stack Exchange** as secondary pending a legal read of the ShareAlike copyleft and the post-2024 access agreement. For Russian and most other locales, there is **no answer** — no licence-clean, author-preserving short-social corpus at scale that I could verify; budget to license or collect your own.

**What would overturn this:** an actual reading of Reddit's/Telegram's current ToS establishing that platform terms override the dataset CC tag for your commercial use — or discovery of a purpose-built, commercially-licensed multilingual social-authorship corpus.

## Q7. Has anything since 2026-08-20 moved the prompting ceiling, and what beats prompting without per-customer fine-tuning?

**Verdict on the one-week window: No. Nothing published in the 2026-08-20 → 2026-08-27 window supersedes PersonalBench's numbers — a one-week window in this field produced nothing that moves the ceiling. On the broader question: no inference-time method is *measured* to reliably beat prompting on author-level similarity without weight updates. Retrieval, contrastive-activation steering, and critic-revise loops all exist and claim gains on personalization *task* benchmarks, but none is validated on a calibrated authorship-verification metric (LUAR-style) to close the human-floor gap. For the length-edit sub-question: prompt-based one-shot exact length control exists (no weight updates), but no published method demonstrates a single surgical length edit that provably preserves author voice.**

Evidence:

- **The ceiling stands.** The theory-grounded result (arXiv:2604.26460, "Theory-Grounded Evaluation Exposes the Authorship Gap in LLM Personalization", v1 dated 29 Apr 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26460) is the anchoring measurement: LUAR human ceiling 0.756, cross-author floor 0.626, all four inference-time methods 0.484–0.508 — below the floor. Nothing in the one-week window overturns it. (See caveat: this is the paper I could verify; the brief's cited PersonalBench arXiv:2608.19746 shares the identical ceiling/floor/method figures.)
- **Retrieval-augmented personalization** ("Optimizing User Profiles via Contextual Bandits for RAG Personalization", ACL 2026, https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1467/; PURPLE; "Improving RAG for Personalization with Author Features and Contrastive Examples", ECIR 2025) reports gains on LaMP/LongLaMP-style *task* metrics — not on a calibrated authorship-verification similarity against a human floor. "Improves personalization benchmarks" ≠ "beats prompting on author similarity." Unproven for your metric.
- **Contrastive activation steering** ("Personalized Text Generation with Contrastive Activation Steering"/StyleVector, ACL 2025, https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.353.pdf; "Style Vectors for Steering Generative LLMs", EACL 2024 Findings, https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-eacl.52/) is inference-time but needs internal activations — **not available through a plain chat API.** Report as "what it would take." Caveats: "On the Limits of Steering Vectors for Preference-Aligned Generation" (arXiv:2607.01802, 2026) finds steering degrades quality when scaled across many traits; the style-vector human evaluation (arXiv:2601.21505, 29 Jan 2026) reports coherence collapse beyond modest steering strength (λ>0.35).
- **Critic-and-revise / self-refine** ("Iterative Critique-Refine Framework for Enhancing LLM Personalization", arXiv:2510.24469): a critic returns feedback on "tone, vocabulary, sentence structure, and topicality," the generator revises, and Knockout/Best-of-N selects stronger drafts — all at inference time, chat-API-compatible. This is the most directly usable non-fine-tuning candidate for you, but it is evaluated on personalization benchmarks, **not** on a calibrated AV similarity closing the human-floor gap. Promising, unproven on your exact metric.
- **"Catch Me If You Can? Not Yet" (arXiv:2509.14543, 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14543)**: LLMs "still struggle to imitate the implicit writing styles of everyday authors" even with RAG and advanced prompting — corroborates that the gap is real and method-agnostic, consistent with your Russian reproduction.
- **Length edit without rewriting voice:** "Prompt-Based One-Shot Exact Length-Controlled Generation with LLMs" (arXiv:2508.13805, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.13805) achieves exact length control by prompting alone, no weight access, on black-box models — directly relevant since your generations run 1.4–5.8× too long. **But** it targets exact length, not voice preservation. That generic "shorten to N" erases voice is corroborated by "Voice Under Revision" (arXiv:2604.22142, 2026, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22142): LLM rewriting "decreases function words and first-person pronouns, while increasing vocabulary diversity, punctuation, and word length … features central to computational stylometry," and voice-preserving instructions "reduce effect magnitude by about one-third but preserve direction" — they help but do not solve it. **No published method demonstrates a single surgical length edit that provably preserves the authorship fingerprint.** Genuine gap.

**What would overturn this:** a paper evaluating a chat-API-compatible method (retrieval, best-of-N with a style reward, or critic-revise) on a calibrated AV metric and showing it reaches or exceeds the human inter-author floor — or a length-control method reporting preserved AV similarity before/after the edit.

## Contradictions kept as contradictions

- **Stack Exchange licence vs access.** The CC BY-SA 4.0 licence says content is to be "shared and remixed" and the ToS permits Stack Overflow to "commercially exploit" it; the post-2024 download agreement says the file is "for projects that do not include training a large language model." Both are true at once — a permissive content licence wrapped in a restrictive access contract. Do not average them; treat the access agreement as an additional, separate constraint.
- **Cross-lingual transfer optimism vs pessimism.** arXiv:2509.16531 finds authorship representations "enjoy cross-lingual transfer" (optimistic); arXiv:2508.01656 finds methods "struggle to generalize across dissimilar language families or writing scripts" (pessimistic). Reconciliation: representation transfers partially, but performance and thresholds do not — the two papers genuinely emphasise opposite halves and should be kept as a pair.
- **Blog Authorship Corpus licence.** Governing source (Koppel's page): non-commercial. Hugging Face mirror: "License: unknown." Kept as a flagged discrepancy — trust the governing source, distrust the mirror.
- **Affix/punctuation n-gram advantage is not universal.** Sapkota et al. and Markov et al. show a clear cross-topic gain in English (+4.7–5.9%); replications report it "does not seem to be valid for the Portuguese language" and may not hold for Turkish. Kept as a script-dependent contradiction rather than a settled rule.

## Recommendations (staged, with change-triggers)

1. **Unblock Q1 by building, not searching.** Implement per-author score normalisation by analogy to adaptive s-norm: for each author, construct a null distribution of Impostors scores (held-out author posts and/or the impostor cohort), then z-score every candidate's Impostors score against that per-author (mean, sd). Report working points in per-author σ-units, not raw percentages, so B's 18.4-point and C's 16.1-point headroom become comparable to A's 57.2. **Label it explicitly as an unvalidated import from speaker verification.** Change-trigger: if validation shows z-scored working points still do not equalise A/B/C separation, fall back to independent per-author operating points chosen per Q4.
2. **Keep 5-grams; change the selection principle and enlarge the budget.** Test affix/punctuation-typed selection (Sapkota) against top-400-by-frequency, at a larger feature budget (order of thousands, capped before Stamatatos's ~3,500 cross-topic ceiling), on your Russian data. Change-trigger: if top-400 frequency matches typed selection on cross-topic AUC, keep it for simplicity. Validate the CJK locale separately — no prior tells you what works there.
3. **Raise the corpus floor.** Keep 15k as a hard minimum but flag 15k–30k as a low-confidence band; prefer ≥30k characters (≈5k Russian words) for a "high-confidence" tier. Change-trigger: a learning curve on your data showing relative-feature AUC of 0.86–0.88 holds at 15k.
4. **Set operating points per locale on adversarial data.** Build each locale's threshold from author-held-out (positive) vs generated (negative) pairs; report false-accept and false-reject as a plain-language 2×2 confusion matrix, and allow a non-answer band (c@1). Never port a threshold across scripts.
5. **Calibration corpus:** adopt Webis-TLDR-17 (CC BY-4.0) for English calibration; hold Stack Exchange as secondary pending a legal read of the copyleft and access agreement. For Russian, budget to collect/license your own.
6. **To beat prompting without fine-tuning:** pilot a critic-and-revise loop (arXiv:2510.24469) and best-of-N using your relative Impostors feature as the selector — both chat-API-compatible — and evaluate on your calibrated relative metric, not on task benchmarks. Treat activation steering as "what it would take" (needs weights/activations, unavailable on a plain chat API).
7. **For the length problem:** use prompt-based one-shot exact length control (arXiv:2508.13805) to hit target length in one pass, then re-score voice with your relative Impostors check; do not use generic "shorten to N." No method guarantees voice preservation, so gate on the post-edit similarity score.

## Caveats

- Most quantitative AV/attribution evidence here is English (Guardian, CCAT, fanfiction, blogs) or literary multilingual (Eder). Russian-specific and CJK-specific measurements for short social posts are largely absent and are treated as gaps, not extrapolated.
- Speaker-verification results (AS-norm, language-matched cohorts, the exact 30% relative improvement) are labelled analogies, not AV evidence.
- PersonalBench is cited in the brief as arXiv:2608.19746 (2026-08-20); the closely related theory-grounded paper I verified is arXiv:2604.26460 (29 Apr 2026). I could not independently open 2608.19746 within budget, but the ceiling/floor figures (0.756 / 0.626; methods 0.484–0.508) match between the brief and the verified 2604.26460 paper.
- Licence claims are quoted from governing documents where reachable (Koppel's page; archive.org license.txt; Hugging Face YAML; Google Dataset Search). The platform-ToS-override question for Reddit and Telegram is a legal determination beyond public dataset cards and is marked unknowable here.
- Two of the four Kestemont/PAN and Impostors primary sources for Q1 (Koppel & Winter's BIU PDF, Potha & Stamatatos, the PAN 2020 overview, and stylo's manual) were verified against full primary text; Kestemont et al. 2016 (Julius Caesar, ESWA 63:86–96) was not opened in full, though the stylo implementation "sticks to" its GI description and its output is a single [0,1] score with no per-author normalisation step.

# Three research prompts for `content-factory-next-pl1`

Run each as a separate deep-research task. Land the answers in `docs/research/`
beside `writer-voice-style-transfer-2026-08-22.md`. If only one is run, run the
first.

**English on purpose.** Stylometry, authorship attribution and style transfer
are almost entirely English-language fields, and a query in another language
does not reach them.

**Multilingual on purpose.** The product ships sixteen interface locales and
English is the primary one. The previous research and the current implementation
were framed Russian-first, and the cost of that is visible in the code: the voice
layer supports two languages and English literally borrows the Russian
dictionaries, so an English corpus is measured with Russian copulas, Russian
deverbal suffixes and Russian function words, and reports zeros that read as
findings. Every prompt below therefore asks the same question along a language
axis: what works with no per-language resource at all, what needs a small list,
and what needs a resource that exists for three languages and not for sixteen.

Split into three because the questions are of different kinds and would blur
into each other in one answer: **what to measure**, **how to make it hold for
any author and any language**, **how to make the model obey it and how to prove
it did**.

## Prompt 1 — What is worth measuring: style dimensions and personality from text

```
You are researching for a product that derives a writer's "brand voice" from
their own texts and then generates new texts in that voice.

LANGUAGE SITUATION. The product ships sixteen interface locales — ar, bn, de,
en, es, fr, he, it, ja, ka (Georgian), ko, pt, ru, tr, vi, zh — and English is
the primary one. The voice feature currently supports only English and Russian,
and English borrows the Russian dictionaries, which is a defect we are fixing.
Assume any of the sixteen may need to work. Treat language portability as a
first-class criterion, not an afterthought.

MATERIAL. Short social/channel posts, 200–4000 characters each; one author's
corpus runs 15,000–150,000 characters, roughly 2,000–20,000 words.

HARD CONSTRAINTS ON ANY RECOMMENDATION.
- The deterministic measurement layer runs in TypeScript in-process, with no
  model call, no morphological parser and no Python service. It must work for a
  workspace whose AI quota is exhausted.
- The product ships under AGPL-3.0. A dependency, model weight, lexicon or norm
  set with an unclear or non-commercial licence cannot be used. State the licence
  for everything you recommend.
- Anything needing a model call is still interesting, but must be labelled as
  such and costed.

WHAT THE PRODUCT MEASURES TODAY. Eight "scales": mean sentence length; spread of
sentence length; share of sentences under 8 words; share of paragraphs containing
a list; share of question sentences; share of clauses where a dash stands in for
a copula; share of first person versus the organisation named from outside; share
of sentences carrying a deverbal noun. Plus relative frequencies of 86 function
words and particles, and character 5-grams. Everything except the character
n-grams needs a per-language word list, and only one language has one.

WHAT WE MEASURED, on a real 153-post Russian channel (131,396 characters). Start
from these numbers rather than re-deriving them; they are why this research
exists. The corpus is Russian, but nothing about the findings is claimed to be
Russian-specific — establishing whether they are is part of the question.
- The eight scales separate the author's held-out posts from another author's
  technical prose in only 48.0% of pairs — a coin flip.
- Character 5-grams separate the same pairs in 94.3%; function words alone 56.4%.
- Against text generated by an LLM on the same author's topics, character
  5-grams drop to 66.7% and function words to 68.7%.
- When the model was handed every measured number as a hard constraint plus
  three of the author's own posts as exemplars, it complied — scales inside their
  corridors rose from 87% to 94% — and the output did not move closer to the
  author stylometrically.

The product owner's reading: the eight scales measure formatting, not voice.
What a reader recognises is whether the writer addresses them informally or
formally, whether they joke, whether they admit their own mistakes, whether they
are blunt or hedging, warm or dry. He calls this "stylistics" and wants the
profile built out of it.

QUESTIONS.

1. Which measurable dimensions of writing style are (a) reliably estimable from
   2,000–20,000 words of short-form text, (b) intelligible to a lay reader with
   no linguistics background, and (c) supported by published validation? For each:
   the exact operationalisation, minimum text, reported reliability, and whether
   it needs a parser or a model. Cover register and dimension frameworks
   explicitly — Biber's multidimensional analysis of register variation and its
   successors, and the cross-linguistic MDA work that has replicated it in other
   languages.

2. SORT EVERY DIMENSION YOU NAME INTO THREE TIERS BY WHAT IT COSTS TO SUPPORT A
   NEW LANGUAGE. This is the most important output of this prompt.
   - Tier A: needs no per-language resource at all — character n-grams,
     punctuation rates, length and structure distributions, and anything else
     that qualifies.
   - Tier B: needs a small closed list a competent speaker can write in a day —
     function words, address forms, hedges, discourse markers. Say how large the
     list has to be and how it is validated.
   - Tier C: needs a heavy resource — psycholinguistic norms, LIWC-style
     dictionaries, trained classifiers. For each, say which of our sixteen
     languages actually has one, at what quality, under what licence.
   Where a dimension exists in more than one tier depending on method, say so.

3. Treat these one by one, because they are what the owner means by stylistics.
   For each: is it measurable deterministically, does it need a model, or is it
   not reliably measurable — and which tier above does it fall into?
   - Formality of address, including languages with a grammatical T–V distinction
     (ru, de, fr, es, it, pt, tr and others) and languages with honorific systems
     (ja, ko) versus English, which marks it lexically and not grammatically.
     How is "addresses the reader informally" measured in a language that has no
     grammatical marker for it?
   - Humour and irony; self-deprecating humour specifically.
   - Directness versus hedging and mitigation; politeness theory and face-work as
     an operationalisable source; and how much of this is culture-bound rather
     than author-bound.
   - Warmth, and the warmth/competence axes from social cognition if they have
     been operationalised on text.
   - Certainty and epistemic stance: hedges, boosters, evidentiality.
   - Concreteness versus abstraction: concreteness and imageability norms, the
     linguistic category model.
   - Admission of one's own error; self-disclosure; self-reference.

4. Personality typing from text. How defensible is it to infer a personality type
   from writing and then steer generation through it? Treat Big Five/OCEAN, MBTI,
   HEXACO and DISC separately. For each: psychometric standing, the size of
   effects actually reported when predicting it from text (correlations, not
   accuracy headlines), how much text is needed, how well it transfers across
   languages, and how well it transfers from essays and long-form to short social
   posts. Include the critical literature — MBTI's test–retest and construct
   validity, and the replication record of language-based Big Five prediction. If
   a typology does not survive scrutiny, say so plainly and say what to use
   instead.

5. Is there published work describing an author's style as a PROFILE OF
   DEVIATIONS from a norm — "jokes more than most people, addresses the reader
   informally, rarely asks questions" — rather than as absolute numbers? Is such
   a description measurably more stable across an author's own texts, more
   portable across languages, or more useful to a human reader?

6. What breaks when a method developed on English is applied to a
   morphologically rich language, a language with free word order, a
   non-alphabetic script, or a right-to-left script? We have all four among our
   sixteen. Which of the dimensions you recommend survive, which need
   reformulation, which do not transfer at all.

OUTPUT FORMAT.
For every question, separate three registers explicitly:
- ESTABLISHED — citation, year, and the actual numbers reported.
- CONTESTED — both sides, and who disagrees with whom.
- FOLKLORE — widely repeated, not supported.
Quote verbatim where the wording matters. Sources may be in any language, and
non-English-language work is wanted where it carries evidence about a language
other than English.
End with: a shortlist of dimensions you would build a product on, ordered by
evidence strength and annotated with their tier from question 2; the minimum kit
needed to add a seventeenth language; and an honest list of gaps. Do not smooth
over a gap by extrapolating from English; if English is all the evidence there
is, say so.
Answer in English.
```

## Prompt 2 — Making the profile hold for any author and any language

```
You are researching for a product that derives a style profile from one author's
own texts and then checks whether a new text reads like that author. The material
is short social/channel posts; one author's corpus is 15,000–150,000 characters.
Authors are arbitrary and unknown in advance: one runs a technical channel,
another a personal diary, another corporate announcements.

LANGUAGE SITUATION. Sixteen interface locales — ar, bn, de, en, es, fr, he, it,
ja, ka, ko, pt, ru, tr, vi, zh — with English primary. The voice feature currently
supports two and one of them borrows the other's dictionaries. Assume any of the
sixteen may need to work, and treat "how much does language N cost" as a question
we need answered, not assumed away.

THE PROBLEM. Every parameter in the product was chosen on a single corpus by a
single author in a single language: the feature (character 5-grams), the profile
size (top 400 n-grams), the corridor percentiles (10th/90th), the floor under the
similarity threshold, the minimum corpus-size rules, and the headline acceptance
number. With one author there is no way to tell a general rule from an overfit
one, and with one language there is no way to tell a general rule from a Russian
one.

One thing is already per-author by construction and we believe it generalises:
the similarity threshold is the 95th percentile of that author's own
leave-one-out distances, not a constant. Tell us if that reasoning is wrong.

QUESTIONS.

1. How does stylometry and authorship verification do AUTHOR-INDEPENDENT
   CALIBRATION? Cover, with evidence about what works at corpus sizes of
   thousands rather than millions of words:
   - normalisation against a background or reference corpus;
   - percentile norms over a population of authors;
   - z-scoring and Burrows's Delta variants, and what the reference set should be;
   - empirical-Bayes and James–Stein shrinkage of per-author estimates on small
     corpora;
   - threshold calibration on a cross-author development set;
   - probability calibration in verification — Platt scaling, isotonic
     regression, and what the PAN authorship-verification shared tasks settled on;
   - the impostors method and the general impostors framework, and whether it
     removes the need for a fixed threshold at all.

2. REFERENCE CORPORA, PER LANGUAGE. To say "this author jokes more than most
   people" we need to know how most people write. For as many of our sixteen
   languages as you can: which corpora of short-form or social text exist, their
   size, composition, collection method, licence, and whether they can be used
   inside an AGPL-3.0 product. Name the multilingual collections that cover many
   at once (Leipzig Corpora Collection, OSCAR, CC-100, mC4, HPLT and whatever
   else is current) and say what their licences actually permit. Where nothing
   licence-clean exists for a language, say so; that is a product decision we
   need to be able to make. Then: how many authors and how much text per author
   does a stable norm require, and what biases must be guarded against when the
   norm comes from web-scraped text?

3. How many authors are needed before a method is validated rather than fitted?
   What cross-author protocols are standard — leave-one-author-out, held-out
   author sets, cross-topic splits? What sample sizes do published papers
   actually use, and how much between-author variance is considered normal? We
   plan to start with three corpora; say honestly what three buys and what it
   does not.

4. CROSS-LINGUAL TRANSFER OF THE METHOD ITSELF. Is there evidence that a
   calibration procedure validated on one language transfers to another, or must
   every language be re-validated from scratch? What do multilingual authorship
   models (mStyleDistance, MSR and successors) report per language, and how much
   does quality vary between a high-resource and a low-resource language in the
   same system? For a product, the practical question is: does adding language N
   mean adding a word list, or adding a whole validation programme?

5. Robustness to GENRE and TOPIC. An author writes about releases one week and a
   holiday the next. What is known about cross-topic and cross-genre stability of
   stylometric features, which features lose it first, and what corrections exist
   — topic masking, function-word-only feature sets, content-word removal, domain
   adaptation?

6. THE RIGHT ADVERSARY. Our measure separates the author from another person's
   technical documentation in 94.3% of pairs and from LLM-generated text on the
   author's own topics in only 66.7%, at equal length; the threshold flags 1 of 24
   generated texts. What is known about deciding "did THIS person write it" when
   the alternative is a competent language model writing on that person's topics —
   as opposed to the much-studied "human or machine" question? Which features
   survive that adversary, is there work on verification against machine-authored
   negatives specifically, and does the answer differ by language?

OUTPUT FORMAT.
For every question separate ESTABLISHED (citation, year, numbers), CONTESTED
(both sides), FOLKLORE. Quote verbatim where wording matters. Sources may be in
any language.
End with a protocol we can execute: how many authors, in how many languages, how
much text, what steps in what order, what numbers count as passing, and what
would falsify the approach. Then an honest list of what the literature does not
answer.
Answer in English.
```

## Prompt 3 — Making a model write in that voice, and proving that it did

```
You are researching for a product that derives a style profile from an author's
own texts and must then generate new texts in that voice. The material is short
social posts. Generation runs through ordinary chat models over an API.
Per-customer fine-tuning is not the assumed path, but we want its cost and payoff
quantified rather than dismissed.

LANGUAGE SITUATION. Sixteen interface locales with English primary; generation
must work in any of them. Where evidence exists only for English, say so
explicitly — that is itself an answer we need.

WHAT WE MEASURED, on a Russian corpus. 24 generations, eight identical neutral
briefs, three variants, every output trimmed to 800 characters before measuring.
Distance is cosine over the author's top-400 character 5-grams; lower is closer.
- The author's own real posts: 0.586.
- Generated with no voice instruction at all: 0.652.
- Generated as the product does today — two enum-derived directives, "write in
  the first person singular" and "keep the register conversational": 0.627.
- Generated with every measured number as a hard constraint plus three of the
  author's own posts quoted in full as exemplars: 0.639.
So hard numeric constraints are obeyed — the share of style scales inside their
corridors rose from 87% to 94% — and buy no additional resemblance.
Also: the author's median post is 823 characters; every variant produced
2,000–2,600 characters, including the one given the length corridor explicitly.

The owner's position, which this research should test rather than assume: hard
corridors are the wrong instrument; the voice should state stylistic direction —
"more of this than most people" — rather than fence the model in.

QUESTIONS.

1. What actually moves an LLM's output toward a specific author's style? Compare
   by effect size and by cost: natural-language style descriptions; numeric
   constraints; in-context exemplars; few-shot versus zero-shot; persona and role
   prompting; retrieval of the author's own similar texts; supervised
   fine-tuning, LoRA, DPO, and demonstration-based methods such as DITTO;
   activation steering and style vectors; decoding-time control. Say for each
   whether the evidence is English-only.

2. RELATIVE versus ABSOLUTE instructions. Is there evidence that "write warmer
   and more direct than usual" outperforms "average sentence length 12 words"?
   How should a stylistic instruction be phrased so a model follows it without
   caricaturing it — turning "jokes sometimes" into a joke per paragraph? Is
   there work on measuring and preventing that overshoot?

3. EXEMPLARS. How many of the author's own texts should go into the prompt, and
   how should they be chosen — nearest the author's centroid, most
   characteristic, most diverse, most similar to the current brief? Is there a
   point at which exemplars start to hurt, through verbatim copying or through
   imitating the sample's content rather than its manner? How is that measured?

4. LENGTH AND STRUCTURE. Our author writes 823-character posts; the model writes
   2,000–2,600 even when told the corridor. What is known about why length
   instructions are poorly followed, and what actually works — token budgets,
   structured output, decoding constraints, iterative shortening, training-time
   fixes? Same question for paragraph and list structure. Does compliance differ
   by output language, given tokenisation differences?

5. STYLE VERSUS SUBSTANCE. The most distinctive habit we measured in this author
   is that he brings his own measured figures — "ran six releases through my own
   rig", "89 points both times" — in 54% of his posts. A model given a topic and
   no facts cannot do this: it has nothing of its own to bring. Is there published
   work on where the boundary between style and content lies in authorship and
   style transfer, and on whether author-level resemblance is attainable without
   the author's own material? This decides a product question, so we need the
   honest answer, including "the literature does not separate these".

6. GENERATION QUALITY ACROSS LANGUAGES. Does style steering hold up equally in a
   model's weaker languages? What is known about instruction-following and style
   control degrading outside English, and what compensates — instructions in
   English with output in the target language, exemplars in the target language,
   or something else?

7. EVALUATION. How should "sounds like this author" be evaluated without
   overfitting the evaluation to one person or one language? Cover
   authorship-verification models as judges (LUAR, STAR, Style Embeddings,
   mStyleDistance, MSR — with language coverage and licences), stylometric
   distances, LLM-as-judge with its known position, verbosity and self-preference
   biases and its documented degradation outside English, and human evaluation.
   What protocols, how many texts, what inter-rater agreement is acceptable?

OUTPUT FORMAT.
For every question separate ESTABLISHED (citation, year, numbers), CONTESTED
(both sides), FOLKLORE. Quote verbatim where wording matters. Sources may be in
any language.
End with a recommended architecture for the voice prompt — what goes in it, in
what order, why each element is there, and what evidence supports each element —
plus an evaluation protocol we can run, and an honest list of gaps.
Answer in English.
```
